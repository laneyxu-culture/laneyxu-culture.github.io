**Laney Xu, Grade 11, St. Francis High School**  
**Feb. 2026**

## Introduction

I first learned about embodied cognition in my AP Psychology class. The basic idea is that thinking isn't just something that happens in your brain—your body also matters. How you move, what you touch, what you feel, all of that shapes how you think.

Around the same time, I was helping organize a Spring Festival Fair at my school. I started wondering whether doing something with your hands actually makes a difference for someone who has never celebrated Chinese New Year before. Is making a dumpling or trying on a hanfu really different from just watching a slideshow?

That question became the starting point for this small study. I didn't have a lab or a research team. I just had a festival, five activities, and a chance to watch what people actually did.

I wanted to look at two things. First, do hands-on cultural activities like cooking or dressing up show signs of embodied cognition or procedural learning? Second, which kinds of activities seem to engage people more?

## Methods

The study took place during a Spring Festival Fair at my high school. About 60 to 80 people came. After the event, I handed out paper questionnaires, and 14 people filled them out and returned them. That's a small number, and I'll come back to why that matters later.

We ran five activities. Dumpling making was about procedural learning and doing steps in order. Hanfu dressing was about enclothed cognition, or how what you wear changes how you hold yourself. Food tasting was more about smell, taste, and memory. Go and Mahjong were both games, so I thought they might involve rules, strategy, and for Mahjong, social coordination.

I watched people as they did each activity and wrote down what I saw. I also used a short questionnaire with rating scales and a few open-ended questions. I told everyone the questionnaire was voluntary and anonymous, and I only collected it if they agreed.

## Results

Dumpling making was probably the most interesting to watch. Almost 70% of first-timers failed to seal the dumpling properly, and filling leaked out. Several people said things like "my hand doesn't know where to go." That moment—when you understand what you're supposed to do but your hand hasn't caught up yet—seems like the heart of procedural learning.

Hanfu dressing also stood out. Five out of seven girls who tried on the Hanfu changed their posture without being told. They walked slower, stood straighter, or adjusted their sleeves more than usual. One said, "I feel like I should sit up straighter." That matches what researchers call enclothed cognition—clothes don't just cover you; they change how you use your body.

For food tasting, nobody hesitated to eat. But many people asked what was in each dish and how it was made. The taste seemed to make them curious about the culture behind it.

Go and Mahjong didn't get many people—maybe 6 to 8 total. For those who did try, it took over ten minutes before they could play even a little on their own. One student said Go "feels more like surrounding than attacking." He wasn't taught that. He figured it out by playing.

Because only 14 people returned the questionnaire, I didn't run any statistics. I just looked at patterns. The average for "how much focus did the activity require" was 4.1 out of 5, and 71% selected 4 or 5. For "did clothing affect your posture or behavior," 57% said yes, which was 8 out of 14. When asked which activity gave the strongest sense of cultural understanding, dumpling making got 36%, the highest.

Some open-ended comments stayed with me. One person wrote, "It's different when you actually have to do it yourself." Another wrote, "Making the dumpling was hard, but I remember it more than the lecture we had last month."

## Discussion

I think different activities show different kinds of procedural learning. Dumpling making produced obvious hand-related frustration. People knew what they were supposed to do, but their hands couldn't do it yet. That feels like one kind of learning. Hanfu dressing was different—it wasn't about skill, it was about posture and movement. People just moved differently without being told. So maybe wearing cultural clothing isn't just a costume; maybe it actually invites you to move like the culture.

The activities people said were most meaningful were the really hands-on ones, like dumpling making. Not food tasting, which is more passive, and not Go or Mahjong, which take a lot of cognitive effort but less obvious body involvement. That fits the idea that embodied experience matters for cross-cultural learning.

There are a lot of limitations though. The questionnaire sample was tiny, only 14 people, so I can't generalize anything. I also didn't do a pre-test, so I don't know how familiar people already were with Chinese culture before the fair. And I was both the organizer and the observer, which probably biased what I noticed and how I interpreted it. Strategy games also didn't work well for a short festival—Go and Mahjong probably need more time and explanation than a single event can provide.

## Conclusion

This wasn't a perfect study. The sample was small, I didn't have a control group, and I was watching my own event. But even with those problems, I saw real things: hands failing at dumpling seals, postures changing inside a Hanfu, people asking about ingredients because of a taste.

For me, the main takeaway is that hands-on cultural activities do seem to trigger observable signs of embodied cognition and procedural learning, especially cooking and dressing up. And a high school festival can be more than a party. With a little planning, it can also be a place to ask real questions about how people think and learn across cultures.

If I did this again, I would collect pre-festival familiarity data, find a way to get more questionnaire responses by making it digital and shorter and giving it out during the event, and have a second person watch and take notes to reduce my own bias.
