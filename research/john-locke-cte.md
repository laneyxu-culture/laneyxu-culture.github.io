When I selected this topic for the John Locke Essay competition, I obviously knew about death, but I had to wrestle with the idea of "life after death." So, I thought, what better way to understand this was to look at the thousands of organ donations that exist around the world every year. The topic of Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE) fascinated me, so I decided to contact the Boston University CTE Center and interview a representative there to understand the relationship between CTE and life after death.

I emailed Adriana Rodriguez, Research Specialist at the Boston University CTE Center. We traded emails back and forth and I asked her a few questions. She willingly shared with me that "brain donors leave behind an incredible legacy by providing us with scientific knowledge" (Rodriguez). This really resonated with me. Individuals diagnosed with this fatal disease are leaving a lasting impact after their death.

I did some research on those affected by CTE and I came across Mike Webster, who was a celebrated professional football player. A monster of a man, he led his team to numerous championships. After retirement, Mike fell on hard times suffering from depression, amnesia, dementia, and cognitive decline. Mike's brain was donated to science, and it was discovered that Mike suffered from CTE. Mike is one of the 32% of athletes who participated in contact sports, suffering from frequent hits to the head (Munakomi and Puckett).

Ms. Rodriguez shared with me that the brain donors along with their families are "absolutely instrumental in the study of CTE" (Rodriguez). Think about the word instrumental. It means the dead were not left in the past, instead, in a way, they are still active participants in science discovery, contributing to the learnings about the complexity of the human brain. Without their organ donations, the living would not be able to advance in research and gain an understanding of this disease. This devastating disease, affecting millions of contact sport athletes and military veterans, can only be diagnosed after death (Munakomi and Puckett). Webster was not the only one who donated his brain to science for further research; thousands of other people have done the same. Our donated brains are examined post-mortem microscopically by specialists in order to determine whether or not they had CTE. Ms. Rodriguez also said in my interview with her that this helps support the argument that organ donations is "a transfer and continuation of self, increasing the importance of the human body after death."

Humans are getting more aware of their continuation of self after death, shown in the significant global rise of organ donations, an increase of 3.3 % in organ transplant is seen in the year 2024 alone ("Organ transplants exceeded 48,000 in 2024; a 3.3 percent increase from the transplants performed in 2023"). More men and women need to donate, not only for organ transplant and research, but also for the continuation of their self-identity and legacy.

Ms. Rodriquez helped me to access more stories about brain donors to their research center on their website. From there, I learned about a young man named Cameron Adamson, only 22 years old at the age of his death.

Cameron was a successful high school wrestler and Marine Corporal. His father, Daryl Adamson said, "Cameron made me laugh, he made me proud and made me honored to be his dad". But before his death at 22, Cameron struggled with suicidal thoughts. He said the world would be better without him. He couldn't sleep. He stopped caring. After he died, his family donated his brain to the BU CTE Center. They found damage in his white matter. His father admitted that knowing more about brain injuries "may have helped us as a family to save Cameron." Still, Daryl keeps his son's legacy alive: "Even if it only saves one person, his death was not the end of his story." (Cabral)

Another devastating example is the story of Jimmy Frayet, who was an English football player who also played and coached in the United States. After his symptoms started to show he became angry unconditionally. His wife started to read about many players from Jimmy's era who were having trouble later in life and some were being diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease or CTE post-mortem. After his death, his brain was donated to the UNITE Brain Bank in 2020. Jimmy was diagnosed with CTE stage IV/IV and Alzheimer's. His brain is stored forever and will continue to be used to advance CTE research and fulfill important milestones, such as diagnosing CTE in living persons. That discovery could save millions and it started with one brain. Jimmy's sons still feel his existence through the influence of his legacies. At a golf tournament, strangers kept approaching his sons, sharing memories of their father (Ferreira).

These stories from actual donors and their families show how we care about our bodies after death because they are our last gift. Cameron's brain might teach a parent to spot the signs. Jimmy's brain might help doctors diagnose CTE before it is too late. Ms. Rodriguez said donors want to leave their loved ones with "answers" (Rodriguez). Cameron and Jimmy did that, and their stories certainly did not end in silence. That is why we care. Because a body is not just a body. It is the final chance for us to say: I was here and I still matter.

What other organs are being donated and explored as related to life after death similar to brain research on conditions like CTE? Research supports the continuing progress of donating organs for further investigation.

The data from organdonor.gov website speaks volumes:
* 103,223 people are waiting for organ transplants in the United States.
* Every eight minutes, another person joins the list.
* Every single day, 16 people on that list die before an organ becomes available.
* 16 people die every day not because they cannot be saved, but because someone did not donate.
* One body can save up to eight lives. One body can heal more than 85 people through tissue and eye donation.

When you sign up to be an organ donor, one body can save up to eight lives ("Organ Donation Statistics"). That means our body -- the same body that held identity, memories and stories -- can provide a stranger in need with a second chance of life. And that is why we care and should care about what happens to our bodies after death. Because buried six feet under the ground or turned to ash, a body saves no one. But if donated, a body becomes the difference between life and death for someone's parents, child or friend. That is a legacy worth leaving.

One of the most impactful areas of donation research is the eyes, particularly cornea donation. Vision loss affects millions of people worldwide ("Blindness and vision impairment"), yet corneal transplants can restore sight in cases that would otherwise be permanent blindness.

The website titled "Saving Sights" refers to a man named Jim. After years from a sudden loss of vision, he received a donated cornea from a deceased donor and regained his ability to see, work, and support his family. Jim was not only a recipient, but also a donor advocate, recognizing the gift he received came from someone who chose to extend their impact beyond death. His experience shows that the eyes of the deceased can literally bring light back into someone's life. Because of that, many people feel that choosing donations reflect compassion and responsibility toward others (Progin). It is the way to create meaning after death. Jim understands this better than anyone. He has walked with families through the worst moments of their lives. He knows that saying yes to donation does not erase grief. But, most importantly, he also knows that it can transform grief into something that saves another family from the same pain. "Pain doesn't have to be wasted," Jim said. "Donation becomes one of those ways meaning is found in the most horrific moments."

Another major area of donation is the heart. Heart transplants are often life-or-death procedures for patients suffering from heart failure. People are growing more aware of heart health. Many students are required to learn CPR before graduating high school ("More than 500 students learn CPR after Duke-led effort"). Unlike many other organs, the heart carries symbolic weight and meaning. It represents identity, emotions, and life itself. Yet, medically, it is a muscle that can be transplanted to sustain someone else's life. A donated heart continues circulation, transferring energy through the new body ("Heart Transplant"). When Cleveland Clinic patient Ryan Zinn got his heart transplant, Ryan firmly believed "that was the beginning of my new life", and that "life has been so full and rich because of what this donation has allowed me to do" ("Meet the Transplant Recipient with Same Heart for 35 Years"). This reinforces the idea that the human body does not lose its value at death, instead, it can directly sustain another human being's existence.

In conclusion, we care about our bodies after death because they still have the power to matter, both in saving lives and in "advancing the study of the disease for years to come" (Rodriguez). As seen in CTE research, the brain continues to contribute to science even after death, helping professionals to study a disease that can only be diagnosed after death. At the same time, organ donation allows the body to function beyond one life -- eyes restore sight, hearts sustain life, and one decision can impact countless futures. As John Locke wrote "the most precious of all possessions is power over ourselves". That power is the most meaningful in the choices we make. A donor may be gone, but their presence remains in the data that leads to medical breakthroughs. Their body is no longer just a memory; it becomes part of an ongoing legacy. Ultimately, death is not simply the end of one's story --- it can become a continuation.

The human body, even at its final moment, still has the power to teach, to heal, and to matter.
